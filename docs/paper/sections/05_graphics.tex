\section{The core: Graphics, Latex and Interpreter}
\label{sec:graphics}

Three libraries of the core turn the results of the analyses into figures and documents, and let them be explored
without compiling a program. Graphics draws histograms, data points, functions and their fits on canvases, on the
screen and in SVG, PDF and PowerPoint files; Latex writes \LaTeX{} documents and Beamer presentations of the canvases;
and Interpreter runs a small command language, used interactively by CapShell. None of them uses ROOT: the drawing,
the vector file formats and the typesetting of the labels are implemented in \CAP{} itself, in standard C++17.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Graphics}
\label{sec:graphicscore}

\paragraph{Drawing model.}
A drawing is described independently of the device that shows it. A \code{Canvas} is the root of a tree of drawable
objects (\code{Drawable2D}): lines, polygons, markers, axes, shapes and, above all, graphs. Each drawable is drawn by
a \code{Renderer}, which turns it into calls to an abstract \code{Painter}: draw a line or a polygon, fill a rectangle,
draw a marker or a text, measure a text. Coordinates passed to a painter are normalised, $(0,0)$ being the lower-left
corner of the canvas and $(1,1)$ its upper-right corner, and each painter maps them onto its own device units. A
renderer never calls a platform: the same code draws a canvas on every device, and what is seen on the
screen is what is written to a file.

The styles follow the conventions of ROOT, so that the styles of existing configurations keep their meaning: colours
are numbers, with the numbering and values of ROOT (\code{kRed+2} is 634), held by a colour table of \CAP{} that does
not need ROOT and gives numbers from 1000 up to any other colour; line, fill and marker styles and font codes are
those of ROOT, the fill styles including its hatch patterns. Colours carry an opacity.

\paragraph{Painters.}
Four painters are provided. \code{SvgPainter} and \code{PdfPainter} write vector files with no external library: the
PDF painter writes the file directly, with the fourteen fonts that every PDF reader has, and can write books of
several pages, one canvas per page. \code{PptxPainter} writes PowerPoint presentations, one slide per canvas, in which
what the renderers draw becomes native shapes (free-form lines and polygons, preset markers, text boxes), so that the
slides can be edited in PowerPoint, Keynote or LibreOffice. \code{ImGuiPainter} draws on the screen for CapGui
(Section~\ref{sec:capgui}). Several canvases can be drawn on one page, each in its own rectangle.

\paragraph{Canvases and graphs.}
A canvas is divided into a grid of $n \times m$ cells, each holding a \code{Graph}: a frame with its axes, title and
legend, set in its cell by margins. A graph shows any number of items (\code{GraphItem}), each a function or a set of
data points (a histogram, a profile or data points, Section~\ref{sec:data}) with its style; the objects are not
copied, and the graph shows them as they are when it is drawn. The ranges are automatic, covering all the items and
rounded to nice values ($1$, $2$ or $5\times 10^k$), unless they are set, and each axis is linear or logarithmic.
Unlike in ROOT, the scales belong to the graph, not to the canvas. The titles of a graph and of its axes are taken from the first object shown (its title
and the titles of its dimensions), and the items get their colours and markers from the palettes of the graph, item
$k$ taking colour and marker $k$. The title, the axis titles and the legend have automatic positions, which can be
moved, and automatic sizes, which follow the size of the cell. Each drawing records where the parts of the graph
went, so that the graph can tell what lies under a point of the canvas: an axis, the title, the legend, a data point
or the residual panel; CapGui uses it for its mouse interactions.

Data points of one variable are drawn as markers with error bars for their statistical uncertainties, and
optionally their uncertainties along $x$ and a line joining them; systematic uncertainties are drawn as boxes around
the points, brackets above and below them, or a band. Functions are drawn as curves over their range.

\paragraph{Maps.}
Items of two variables, functions $f(x,y)$, histograms of rank two and data points with a value, are drawn as colour
maps. Their values are put on the cells of a grid (\code{Grid2D}): the bins of a histogram, the nodes of points that
form a regular grid, or the centres of $n \times n$ cells over which a function is evaluated, over the part of its
range that is visible, so that zooming shows more detail; scattered points, which form no grid, are drawn as markers
coloured by their value. The graph then has a third axis, $z$, drawn along a colour scale beside the frame, linear or
logarithmic, whose range is automatic or set, and optionally symmetric about zero. A palette (\code{Palette}) gives
the colour of each value: \emph{viridis} by default, perceptually uniform and readable in grey and by colour-blind
readers, the default palette of ROOT, a grey scale for printing, and a diverging palette (blue, white, red) for
values of both signs, such as correlation functions; with a number of steps, the palette shows filled contours.
Contour lines are traced on the cells by marching squares, at equally spaced levels or at given levels, and the values
can be written in the cells where they fit, or drawn as disks whose area follows their magnitude. Maps and curves can
be shown together, for instance a curve over a map.

\paragraph{Three-dimensional views.}
A graph can show its maps in a three-dimensional view: a box with the $x$, $y$ and $z$ axes, seen from a direction
given by an azimuth and an elevation, in orthographic projection (\code{Projection3D}). Each map is drawn as a surface
shaded by the palette, as a mesh with its hidden lines removed, or as lego boxes, the parts being drawn from the
farthest to the nearest. The view is turned with the mouse in CapGui.

\paragraph{Fits.}
The fits attached to an object (Section~\ref{sec:fitting}) are drawn with it, each with its own line, over its range
or over the whole graph, and listed in the legend under the object, optionally with their quality
($\chi^2/\nu$ or $-2\ln L$). Fits of rank two are drawn as contour lines over the map, at the levels of its
contours, or as meshes in a three-dimensional view. A panel below the frame can show the residuals of the fits,
$y_i - f(x_i)$ with their error bars or the pulls $\bigl(y_i - f(x_i)\bigr)/\sigma_i$, sharing the $x$ axis of the
frame; for fits of two variables, the map of the pulls is drawn with the diverging palette and a range symmetric
about zero (Fig.~\ref{fig:graphics}).

\paragraph{Palettes, shapes and texts.}
The palettes of a session (\code{Palettes}) are named: colour palettes (a default one, one safe for colour-blind
readers, and one for the curves of the fits), marker palettes (filled or open markers) and the shading palettes of the
maps; programs add their own. Each graph uses the current palettes of the session unless it names its own, so that
changing a palette of the session restyles every graph that follows it. Shapes (\code{Shape}: rectangles, ellipses,
triangles, polygons, lines and arrows, and text boxes) are drawn over a graph to annotate it; their positions are
fractions of the cell of the graph, so that they keep their place when the axes are zoomed or the canvas is resized,
and they can be filled, hatched, outlined and hold a text.

The texts of the figures (titles, axis titles, legends, labels of shapes) are typeset by \LaTeX{} when they contain
\verb|$| or \verb|\|, for instance \verb|$p_{\mathrm{T}}$ (GeV/$c$)|; texts in the notation of the \code{TLatex}
class of ROOT (\verb|#Delta#varphi|, \verb|p_{T}|) are translated to \LaTeX{} first, so that the titles of existing
histograms are typeset too. \LaTeX{} is a program, not a library: \code{LatexRenderer} runs \code{latex} and
\code{dvisvgm} of the \LaTeX{} installation of the system on a small document, and keeps the glyphs of the result as
vector paths, which every painter scales to the size of the text and draws as such (as paths in SVG and PDF files),
so that the texts of the figures look like those of the paper they go into. Each typesetting takes about half a
second, so the results are cached, in memory and on disk, and a text is typeset only once; the texts of a canvas
are typeset together in a single run, and an interface asks for them in a background thread and draws plain text
until they are ready. Without \LaTeX{}, the texts are drawn with the fonts of the painters, the \LaTeX{} and ROOT
notations being turned into Unicode characters, superscripts and subscripts, as they are for PowerPoint.

\paragraph{The canvases of a session.}
The canvases of a session are listed, by name and by group (a window, a step of an analysis), in a registry
(\code{CanvasRegistry}), from which every canvas, and through it every graph, is reached by a program, CapShell or
CapGui; the registry writes them all, one file per canvas or per graph, or as a book of one page per canvas. A
figure is made in a few lines:
\begin{widetext}
\begin{verbatim}[language=C++]
CAP::GRAPHICS::Canvas canvas("spectra", "Transverse momentum spectra", 800, 600);
canvas.divide(2, 1);                                        // two graphs side by side
CAP::GRAPHICS::Graph & left = canvas.graph(0);
left.show(CAP::GRAPHICS::GraphItem(&ptPions, "$\\pi^{+}$"));
left.show(CAP::GRAPHICS::GraphItem(&ptKaons, "$K^{+}$"));           // overlaid
left.setLogY(true);
canvas.graph(1).show(CAP::GRAPHICS::GraphItem(&r2, "$R_2$"));        // a map
CAP::GRAPHICS::PdfPainter pdf(800, 600);
canvas.draw(pdf);
pdf.write("spectra.pdf");
\end{verbatim}
\end{widetext}

\paragraph{Plot files.}
The windows, canvases and graphs of a figure can also be described in an XML file, a \emph{plot file}, read and
written by \code{PlotSpec}: its inputs (CAP files), its outputs, and for each graph the histograms and functions it
shows and every setting of style that differs from the defaults. \code{PlotTemplate} makes plot files from the
histograms of CAP files or of a workflow, laid out by task, histogram and combination of filters, and
\code{PlotDocument} edits them, keeping their structure valid, with undo and redo. Plot files are what CapGui shows
and saves, and what \code{PlotHistos} draws in batch; they are described with CapGui
(Section~\ref{sec:plotfiles}).

\subsection{Documents}
\label{sec:latex}

The Latex library writes \LaTeX{} documents from C++: a \code{LatexDocument} is a tree of elements (sections,
subsections, paragraphs, texts, equations, lists, figures and groups of figures, and the frames of a Beamer
presentation), with its packages, commands, title, authors and abstract, written as a \code{.tex} file. It is how
the results of an analysis are put into documents without being copied by hand. \code{CanvasPresentation} makes a
Beamer presentation of canvases: each canvas, or each of its graphs, is written as a PDF figure and shown on a frame
of its own, titled after the canvas, and the document is compiled with \code{pdflatex}:
\begin{widetext}
\begin{verbatim}[language=C++]
CAP::CanvasPresentation talk("Two-particle correlations");
talk.setAuthor("C. Pruneau", "", "Wayne State University");
talk.addCanvases(CAP::GRAPHICS::CanvasRegistry::session());
CAP::String tex = talk.write("talk", "correlations");
talk.compile(tex);                    // talk/correlations.pdf
\end{verbatim}
\end{widetext}
The outputs of a plot file include such a document, either a Beamer presentation with one frame per canvas or an
article with one section per window and one figure per canvas, written by CapGui and \code{PlotHistos}
(Section~\ref{sec:plotfiles}), next to the PDF book and the PowerPoint presentation of the same canvases.

\subsection{CapShell}
\label{sec:capshell}

\paragraph{The language.}
CapShell runs the command language of the Interpreter library, meant for exploring results and making figures
without writing and compiling a program, as ROOT's prompt is used, but with a language of its own rather than C++.
It has variables, made by assignment; integers, reals, texts, booleans, \code{none} and lists; arithmetic (integers
stay integers through $+$, $-$, $\times$ and powers, and a division always gives a real), comparisons and logical
operators; ranges \code{a..b}; blocks, \code{if}--\code{else}, \code{while} and \code{for} loops over ranges and lists,
with \code{break} and \code{continue}; and functions, defined with \code{fun}, which see their parameters and read and
assign the global variables. The objects are those of the classes of the reflection registry
(Section~\ref{sec:objects}): an object is made by calling its class, and used through its member functions and
properties, so that every class registered becomes available to the language without further code. Histograms, data
points, random generators, canvases and graphs are registered today.

\paragraph{Built-in functions.}
The built-in functions write values, give help on the language, on any function and on any registered class, list
the classes and the variables, run files of commands, time them, and convert and combine values (numbers, texts,
lists, sums, extrema and the usual mathematical functions). Others reach the files and the graphics: \code{ls},
\code{read} and \code{write} list, read and write the objects of CAP files; \code{save} draws objects in one graph and
writes it to an SVG or PDF file; and the canvases of the session are found by name or index, registered, saved one
file per canvas or per graph, printed as a book, or shown in a Beamer presentation, compiled at once
(Section~\ref{sec:latex}):
\begin{verbatim}
g = MersenneTwister(12345)
h = Histogram("h", "Gaussian numbers", 40, -4, 4)
for k in 1..10000 { h.fill(g.gauss(0, 1)) }
if h.mean(0) > 0 { print("positive mean", h.mean(0)) }
save("gauss.pdf", h)
pt = read("run.cap", "Single/MB_PiP/n1_pt")
c = Canvas("spectra", "Spectra", 800, 600)
c.divide(1, 1)
c.graph(0).add(pt)
c.graph(0).logY = true
c.save("pt.pdf")
\end{verbatim}

\paragraph{Sessions.}
\code{CapShell} runs an interactive session, with line editing and a history kept between sessions when the
\code{editline} library is present, runs files of commands and stops, or runs a file and continues interactively;
commands are also read from the standard input, so that the program can be used in pipes and scripts. An error
reports the file and the line where it occurred; in an interactive session, the session goes on.
